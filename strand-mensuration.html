E5 Decision Tree: Sectors, Cones, Spheres & Compound Solids

CIE gives cone, sphere, pyramid, and cylinder volume formulas on Page 2—but arc length, sector area, segment area, and flat base circles are NOT given.

1. Arcs, Sectors & Shaded Segments

  • Arc length: $L = \dfrac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r$
  • Perimeter of a sector: $\text{Arc length} + 2r$ (never forget the two straight radii!).
  • Sector area: $A_{\text{sector}} = \dfrac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2$
  • Minor segment area: $A_{\text{sector}} - \frac{1}{2}r^2\sin\theta$.

2. Cones, Sectors Folded into Cones & Frustums

  • Cone slant height: $l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$.
  • Sector folded into a cone: Sector radius $R \to$ slant height $l$; Sector arc length $\to$ base circumference $2\pi r$.
  • Frustum: $\text{Large Cone} - \text{Removed Small Cone}$ (use similar triangles $\frac{r_1}{r_2} = \frac{h_1}{h_2}$).

3. Compound Solids (Surface Area vs Volume)

  • Volume: Simply ADD (or subtract if hollow) the volumes of the individual parts.
  • Total Surface Area: Only count exposed outer faces—do NOT include hidden internal joins! (e.g. Hemisphere on cylinder $= 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h + \pi r^2$).

4. Melting / Recasting & Unit Conversions

  • Melting/pouring liquid: Volume is conserved ($\text{Volume}_{\text{before}} = \text{Volume}_{\text{after}}$); $\pi$ usually cancels cleanly.
  • Units: $1\text{ m}^2 = 10\,000\text{ cm}^2$; $\quad 1\text{ m}^3 = 1\,000\,000\text{ cm}^3$; $\quad 1\text{ litre} = 1000\text{ cm}^3$.

High-Frequency CIE Examiner Traps

  • Using perpendicular height $h$ instead of slant height $l$ in $\pi r l$: The formula sheet gives curved surface area of a cone as $\pi r l$. Always use Pythagoras $l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$ first if only $h$ is given.
  • Hemisphere surface area: A solid hemisphere has curved area $\frac{1}{2}(4\pi r^2) = 2\pi r^2$, PLUS a flat circular base $\pi r^2$ ($= 3\pi r^2$ total) if it stands alone—but NOT if that base is glued onto a cylinder!
  • Exact $\pi$ on Paper 2: When the question says "Give your answer in terms of $\pi$", keep $\pi$ algebraic right through to the final line.
